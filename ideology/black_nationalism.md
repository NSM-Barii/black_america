# Black Nationalism — The Philosophy

Most people hear "black nationalism" and think it means black people hating white people. That is a deliberate mischaracterization designed to discredit the idea without engaging with it. Here is what it actually is.

---

## The Core Idea

Black nationalism at its foundation is this:

Black people constitute a nation — a people with a shared history, shared culture, shared ancestry, and shared oppression — and that nation has the right to self-determination. The right to define itself, govern itself, and control its own destiny without waiting for the approval or cooperation of white America.

That is it. That is the core.

Everything else — separatism, Pan-Africanism, economic nationalism, cultural nationalism — are different expressions of that central idea.

---

## Why It Emerged

Black nationalism didn't emerge from hatred. It emerged from a specific historical experience and a specific political conclusion:

That American democracy was not designed for black people and had repeatedly demonstrated it would not include black people voluntarily.

By the time serious black nationalist thought developed in the 19th and early 20th century black people had experienced:
- 246 years of chattel slavery
- The betrayal of Reconstruction — the brief period after the Civil War when black people gained political rights, then had them violently stripped away
- Lynching as a tool of racial terror — over 4,000 documented lynchings between 1877 and 1950
- Legal segregation enforced by the state
- Systematic exclusion from economic opportunity

The political conclusion some reached was this — integration into a system that was built on your subjugation is not liberation. It is assimilation into your own oppression.

Black nationalism was the rejection of that path.

---

## The Key Thinkers — In Order

### [[Martin Delany]] — 1812–1885

The grandfather of black nationalism. Before Marcus Garvey, before Malcolm X, there was Martin Delany.

Delany was a free black man, physician, and the first black field officer in the United States Army. He argued in the 1850s that black people had no future in America and should emigrate to Africa or Latin America to build an independent black nation.

His core argument: you cannot be truly free in a country that was built on your enslavement. Freedom requires land, sovereignty, and self-governance — none of which black people had in America.

He was largely forgotten by mainstream history. He shouldn't be.

---

### [[Marcus Garvey]] — 1887–1940

The most important black nationalist figure before Malcolm X. Possibly the most influential black leader in history in terms of mass movement building.

Garvey was born in Jamaica and founded the **Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA)** in 1914. At its peak in the 1920s Garvey claimed **6 million members** across the United States, Caribbean, and Africa — historians estimate the real figure closer to 1–2 million. Still the largest black mass movement in history up to that point.

His philosophy — Pan-Africanism and black nationalism combined:
- Black people worldwide are one people connected by African ancestry and shared oppression
- Africa is the homeland and black people should return to build a great African nation
- Black economic independence is essential — stop spending money with people who oppress you
- Black pride, black beauty, black self-sufficiency are non-negotiable

His famous declaration: **"Up you mighty race, you can accomplish what you will."**

He founded the **Black Star Line** — a shipping company owned and operated by black people, funded by black investors. The idea was economic independence and literal transportation back to Africa. It ultimately failed due to mismanagement and sabotage by the US government — J. Edgar Hoover specifically targeted Garvey and had him deported.

**Why Garvey matters for Malcolm X:**
Malcolm X's father Earl Little was a Garveyite — a devoted follower of Marcus Garvey. Malcolm grew up hearing Garvey's ideas at home. The foundation of his worldview was laid before he ever encountered the Nation of Islam. When you read the autobiography and Malcolm talks about his father — you are reading about Garveyism's direct influence on his formation.

---

### [[W.E.B. Du Bois]] — 1868–1963

Du Bois was not a black nationalist in the Garvey sense — he believed in integration and working within American democratic institutions. But he is essential to understand because his debate with Garvey defines the central tension in black political thought that still exists today.

**Du Bois argued:**
- Black people are American and have earned their place here through centuries of labor and sacrifice
- The goal should be full integration and equal citizenship
- Black intellectual excellence — the "Talented Tenth" — should lead the race upward within American society
- Pan-Africanism yes, but separation no

**Garvey vs Du Bois was brutal.** They genuinely despised each other. Garvey called Du Bois "a white man's n*gger." Du Bois helped the government build a case that got Garvey deported.

This wasn't just personal — it was a fundamental disagreement about what black liberation required.

That same disagreement — integration vs. separation, work within the system vs. build outside it — is the fault line that runs through all of black political thought including Malcolm X vs. Martin Luther King Jr.

---

### [[Elijah Muhammad]] and The Nation of Islam

The Nation of Islam was a specific expression of black nationalist philosophy dressed in a religious framework.

The NOI taught:
- Black people are the original people of the earth
- White people were created by an evil scientist named Yakub — a deliberate mythological inversion of white supremacist ideology
- Integration with white America was impossible and undesirable
- Black people needed complete separation — their own nation, their own economy, their own institutions

This is where Malcolm X found his intellectual and political home — temporarily.

---

## The Central Tension in Black Nationalism

Every black nationalist thinker wrestles with the same unresolved questions:

**Separation or integration?**
Do black people build completely separately from white America or do they fight for full inclusion within it?

**Nationalism or Pan-Africanism?**
Is the goal black Americans specifically or black people globally as one connected diaspora?

**Reform or revolution?**
Do you work within existing systems to change them or do you reject those systems entirely as illegitimate?

Malcolm X's genius — and his evolution — was that his answers to these questions changed as he grew. The early Malcolm gave one set of answers. The post-Mecca Malcolm was moving toward different ones entirely when he was assassinated.

That evolution is what makes him one of the most important thinkers in American history.

**Martin Delany**
- Martin Delany, *The Condition, Elevation, Emigration and Destiny of the Colored People of the United States* (1852) — primary source; Delany's foundational black nationalist argument
- Cyril Griffith, *The African Dream: Martin R. Delany and the Emergence of Pan-African Thought* (1975)

**Marcus Garvey**
- Marcus Garvey, *Philosophy and Opinions of Marcus Garvey* (1923, edited by Amy Jacques Garvey) — primary source; Garvey's own writings and speeches
- Colin Grant, *Negro with a Hat: The Rise and Fall of Marcus Garvey* (2008) — the most thorough modern biography; source for the corrected membership estimates
- Theodore Vincent, *Black Power and the Garvey Movement* (1971)

**W.E.B. Du Bois**
- W.E.B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) — primary source; foundational text including the "Talented Tenth" argument
- W.E.B. Du Bois, *Dusk of Dawn* (1940) — Du Bois's intellectual autobiography

**On the Garvey vs Du Bois Conflict**
- David Levering Lewis, *W.E.B. Du Bois: The Fight for Equality and the American Century* (2000) — Pulitzer Prize-winning biography

**On the Nation of Islam**
- Elijah Muhammad, *Message to the Blackman in America* (1965) — primary source; NOI theology and political philosophy
- Claude Andrew Clegg III, *An Original Man: The Life and Times of Elijah Muhammad* (1997)
