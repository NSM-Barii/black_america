# The AME Church and What Black People Built

## What the AME Church Did for Black People

Richard Allen founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church in 1816 — the first fully independent Black institution in America. Everything it built came from that one act of ownership.

**Education**
The AME Church was the first institution that systematically taught Black people to read at a time when literacy was illegal for enslaved people across the South. It founded Wilberforce University in Ohio in 1856 — the first university in America owned and operated by Black people.

**The Underground Railroad**
AME churches were safe houses and hiding stations for escaped slaves. The legal independence Allen had won in court meant no white authority could walk in and take control. That autonomy directly protected freedom seekers moving north.

**Reconstruction**
After the Civil War AME ministers ran for political office, organized Black voters, and built the political infrastructure that made Black participation in American democracy briefly possible. The church was the organizing center of Black political life.

**The Civil Rights Movement**
Every mass meeting, every strategy session, every organizing drive of the Civil Rights Movement happened in Black churches. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference — Martin Luther King Jr.'s organization — operated entirely through Black church networks. The AME was the backbone of that infrastructure.

**Community Survival**
The AME Church functioned simultaneously as:
- A school
- A bank
- A mutual aid society
- An employment network
- A political organization
- A safe space where Black people could be fully human

Every institution Black people needed and were denied access to — the AME built it and ran it themselves.

## What Black People Built — and What Was Destroyed

### Black Wall Street — Tulsa, Oklahoma — 1921
The Greenwood District of Tulsa was the wealthiest Black community in American history. Known as Black Wall Street — it contained:
- Over 300 Black owned businesses
- 21 churches
- 21 restaurants
- 30 grocery stores
- 2 Black owned movie theaters
- A hospital
- A bank
- A post office
- Law offices, hotels, jewelry stores

On May 31 — June 1, 1921 a white mob supported by local government and law enforcement burned it to the ground. Over 300 Black people killed. 35 blocks destroyed. 10,000 Black residents left homeless. Airplanes were used to drop firebombs and shoot at Black residents from above.

The official death toll was suppressed for decades. The survivors were never compensated. The land was seized.

It was rebuilt — smaller — by the community. Then destroyed again by urban renewal policies in the 1960s.

### Reconstruction — 1865-1877
After the Civil War Black people built an entire political and economic infrastructure across the South:
- 2,000 Black men held public office including senators and congressmen
- Black communities built schools, churches, and businesses
- Black land ownership grew rapidly

In 1877 the federal government withdrew from the South as part of a political compromise. Without federal protection the former Confederacy immediately dismantled everything Black people had built through violence, intimidation, and law. Reconstruction was over in 12 years. Jim Crow replaced it for the next 100.

### The Sea Islands — South Carolina — 1861-1865
During the Civil War enslaved people on the Sea Islands of South Carolina were abandoned by their Confederate owners. They established self-governing communities, farmed the land collectively, built schools, and created functioning independent societies.

After the war General Sherman issued Special Field Order No. 15 — 40 acres of confiscated Confederate land to each freed family. Approximately 40,000 Black people settled on 400,000 acres.

President Andrew Johnson overturned the order months later and returned the land to Confederate owners. The freed people were removed by force.

This is where "40 acres and a mule" comes from — a promise made and immediately broken.

### Rosewood — Florida — 1923
Rosewood was a prosperous Black town in Florida. On January 1, 1923 a white mob from a neighboring town attacked based on a false accusation. They burned every Black owned structure in the town to the ground. At least 6 Black people were killed — the real number is likely higher. The entire Black population fled and never returned.

The state of Florida did not acknowledge what happened until 1994 — 71 years later.

### The Freedmen's Bureau — 1865-1872
Established after the Civil War to help formerly enslaved people transition to freedom — providing food, housing, education, legal assistance, and helping negotiate labor contracts.

It established over 1,000 schools and several historically Black colleges and universities including Howard University and Fisk University.

Congress defunded and abolished it in 1872 under pressure from Southern states. The work it was doing — building Black economic and educational infrastructure — was exactly what the former Confederacy wanted stopped.

### Bruce's Beach — Manhattan Beach, California — 1912-1924
Willa and Charles Bruce purchased beachfront property in Manhattan Beach, California in 1912 and built a resort for Black families who were excluded from every other beach in the area. It became a thriving community destination.

The city of Manhattan Beach used eminent domain in 1924 to seize the property — claiming they needed it for a park. No park was built. The land sat empty for decades.

The Bruce family received no compensation at market value. The land was eventually returned to their descendants in 2021 — 97 years later.

## The Pattern

Every time Black people built something — a community, a political system, an economic base, an institution — a new Pharaoh came and dismantled it.

Black Wall Street. Reconstruction. The Sea Islands. Rosewood. The Freedmen's Bureau.

The destruction was never random. It was targeted specifically at Black success because Black success was a direct challenge to the idea that Black people were inferior and deserved their condition.

The AME Church survived because Allen had the legal foresight to secure its independence in court before anyone tried to take it. He built something they couldn't burn down because he made sure it was legally untouchable.

That's why ownership matters. That's why institution building matters. That's why the AME Church is still standing 230 years later when everything else was destroyed.
