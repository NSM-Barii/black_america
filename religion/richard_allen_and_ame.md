# Richard Allen and the African Methodist Episcopal Church

## Who Was Richard Allen

Richard Allen was born into slavery on February 14, 1760 in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. He was owned by Benjamin Chew, a prominent Philadelphia lawyer, and later sold along with his family to a farmer named Stokeley Sturgis in Delaware.

At approximately 17 years old Allen attended a Methodist meeting and converted. His slaveholder Sturgis attended the same meetings and was also converted — and under the weight of Wesley's theology that slavery was a sin, Sturgis allowed Allen and his brother to purchase their own freedom.

Allen worked multiple jobs simultaneously — sawing wood, hauling salt, working in a brickyard — and purchased his freedom around 1780 at approximately 20 years old.

He became a Methodist preacher, traveling extensively through the Mid-Atlantic states, and was eventually invited to preach at St. George's Methodist Episcopal Church in Philadelphia — one of the most prominent Methodist congregations in America.

---

## The Incident at St. George's — 1787

In November 1787 Richard Allen, Absalom Jones, and other Black members of St. George's knelt in prayer during a service. A white church officer approached them mid-prayer and told them they were in the wrong section — they had unknowingly knelt in a section newly designated for white worshippers only.

They were told to move. They refused until the prayer was finished. When it ended Allen, Jones, and the other Black members stood up and walked out of St. George's together.

Allen later wrote:

*"We all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more plagued with us in the church."*

He never returned.

## What Allen Built

### Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church — 1794

Allen founded Bethel AME Church in Philadelphia in 1794. He purchased a blacksmith's shop, had it converted into a church, and consecrated it as a place of worship fully owned and controlled by Black people.

The legal fight to secure that ownership was long and bitter. The white Methodist establishment repeatedly attempted to assert control over Bethel's property and leadership. Allen fought them in court for years and won.

In 1816 the Pennsylvania Supreme Court ruled that Bethel AME was legally independent — no white Methodist authority had any claim over it.

### The African Methodist Episcopal Church — 1816

On April 9, 1816 — the same year the Pennsylvania Supreme Court secured Bethel's independence — representatives from Black Methodist congregations across the Mid-Atlantic met in Philadelphia and formally established the **African Methodist Episcopal Church** as an independent denomination.

Richard Allen was elected its first bishop.

He was 56 years old. He had been free for 36 years. He had built the first fully independent Black institution in American history from a converted blacksmith's shop.

## What the AME Has Achieved

### The Underground Railroad
AME churches were primary stations on the Underground Railroad. The institution Allen built to be independent of white oversight was also structurally positioned to hide and move enslaved people without white authorities knowing. The autonomy Allen fought for in court directly enabled abolitionist work.

### Denmark Vesey — 1822
Denmark Vesey was a founding member of Emanuel AME Church in Charleston, South Carolina — the church he used to organize what would have been the largest slave rebellion in American history. When the plot was discovered the white authorities of South Carolina didn't just execute Vesey — they demolished the Emanuel AME Church entirely.

They understood what the institution represented.

### Education
From its founding the AME Church built schools. Before public education existed for Black Americans the AME Church was educating Black children. Wilberforce University in Ohio — founded 1856 — was the first university owned and operated by Black Americans. It was an AME institution.

### Reconstruction
After the Civil War AME ministers ran for office, organized politically, and built institutions across the South. The AME Church was the organizational infrastructure of Black political life during Reconstruction.

### The Civil Rights Movement
Every major Civil Rights organization had deep roots in the Black church generally and the AME Church specifically. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference — Martin Luther King Jr.'s organization — operated through Black church networks. Mass meetings, organizing drives, and strategy sessions all happened in Black churches.

### Emanuel AME — 2015
On June 17, 2015 a white supremacist named Dylann Roof entered Emanuel AME Church in Charleston, South Carolina — the same congregation Denmark Vesey had founded — sat through a Bible study session, and murdered 9 people.

He specifically chose Emanuel AME. In his manifesto he wrote that Black people were "taking over the world" and that someone had to do something. He chose the oldest and most historically significant Black church in the South as his target.

A white supremacist in 2015 still considered the institution Richard Allen founded in 1794 dangerous enough to attack. That is the measure of what Allen built.

## The AME Church Today

- **Founded**: 1816
- **Founder**: Bishop Richard Allen
- **Headquarters**: Nashville, Tennessee
- **Congregations**: Approximately 3,000 in the United States
- **Members**: Approximately 2.5 million worldwide
- **Presence**: United States, Africa, Caribbean, Europe, South America
- **Current bishop**: The AME Church has multiple bishops overseeing different districts — it is governed collectively, not by a single leader
- **Wilberforce University**: Still operating in Ohio — the oldest historically Black university in America
- **Notable members throughout history**: Harriet Tubman, Denmark Vesey, Benjamin Tucker Tanner, Reverend Al Sharpton, numerous Civil Rights leaders

---

## Richard Allen's Death and Legacy

Richard Allen died on March 26, 1831 in Philadelphia. He was 71 years old.

He was born enslaved. He died the bishop of a denomination with tens of thousands of members, the founder of the first fully independent Black institution in America, and the man who proved that Black people could build and sustain their own institutions completely outside of white oversight.

He did not wait for white America to give him space. He walked out, bought a blacksmith's shop, and built something that has outlasted every person who tried to stop him by over 200 years.
