# John Wesley and the Origins of Methodism

To understand Richard Allen and the AME Church you have to understand John Wesley. Allen didn't build from nothing — he took an existing tradition, stripped it of its compromises, and made it fully his own.

---

## Who Was John Wesley

John Wesley was born in England in 1703, the son of an Anglican minister. He studied at Oxford, was ordained as a priest in the Church of England, and in the 1730s founded a small group at Oxford that his peers mockingly called the "Methodists" — because of the methodical, disciplined way they practiced their faith.

The name stuck.

Wesley spent his life preaching outdoors to people the Church of England ignored — coal miners, factory workers, the urban poor. He rode on horseback an estimated 250,000 miles across England and America preaching to anyone who would listen. He preached over 40,000 sermons in his lifetime.

His theology had two central pillars:

**1. Grace is available to everyone.**
Wesley rejected the Calvinist doctrine of predestination — the idea that God had predetermined who would be saved and who would be damned. Wesley believed salvation was available to every human being without exception. No one was excluded.

**2. Faith must produce action.**
Wesley believed that genuine Christian faith could not be passive. It had to produce works — caring for the poor, visiting prisoners, feeding the hungry. Christianity that did not engage with human suffering was not Christianity at all.

These two ideas — universal grace and active faith — had radical implications. If grace is available to everyone, then no human being is beneath God's concern. If faith must produce action, then ignoring injustice is a sin.

## Wesley and Slavery

John Wesley was one of the earliest and most forceful opponents of the transatlantic slave trade in the English-speaking world.

In 1774 he published **"Thoughts Upon Slavery"** — a direct, unambiguous condemnation of the entire slave system. He called it:

*"That execrable sum of all villainies commonly called the Slave Trade."*

He argued that:
- No human being could be legitimately owned as property
- The slave trade was built on kidnapping, which the Bible condemned
- Christians who participated in or profited from slavery were committing a grave sin against God
- The argument that Africans were inferior or savage was a lie invented to justify profit

On his deathbed in 1791 Wesley wrote his final letter — addressed to William Wilberforce, the British abolitionist fighting to end the slave trade in Parliament:

*"Unless the divine power has raised you up to be as Athanasius contra mundum, I see not how you can go through your glorious enterprise in opposing that execrable villainy which is the scandal of religion, of England, and of human nature. Unless God has raised you up for this very thing, you will be worn out by the opposition of men and devils. But if God be for you, who can be against you?"*

He died six days later. Wilberforce succeeded in abolishing the British slave trade in 1807 — the same year the Slave Bible was produced.

Wesley's abolitionism was not a footnote. It was central to his theology. You cannot separate the man's faith from his opposition to slavery — they came from the same source.

## How American Methodism Betrayed Wesley

Wesley's movement spread rapidly to the American colonies in the 1760s and 1770s. After the American Revolution Methodism became one of the fastest growing denominations in the new nation.

And then it compromised.

As Methodism spread through the American South it encountered a problem — the slaveholding class. Southern planters were potential converts. Their money built churches. Their communities shaped congregations. And they were not going to join a denomination that called their entire economic system a sin.

So American Methodism made a series of accommodations:

- The Methodist Episcopal Church initially condemned slavery in 1784 but walked it back almost immediately under pressure from Southern members
- By the early 1800s the denomination had effectively dropped active abolitionism as a requirement
- Southern Methodist ministers were preaching Ephesians 6:5 from the same pulpits Wesley had intended for the liberation of the poor
- In 1844 the denomination formally split — **Methodist Episcopal Church** in the North and **Methodist Episcopal Church South** in the South — specifically over whether a bishop who owned slaves could remain in leadership

The Southern branch did not reunite with the Northern branch until 1939. By that point it had spent nearly a century as the theological backbone of the slaveholding South.

This is the institution Richard Allen walked out of in 1787.

---

## What Allen Saw

Allen was a Methodist. He believed in Wesley's theology — universal grace, active faith, the obligation to care for the poor and oppressed. He had converted to Methodism and built his faith on its foundations.

But the American Methodist church he attended was practicing something Wesley would not have recognized. It was segregating Black worshippers during prayer. It was accommodating slaveholders. It was drifting toward the exact compromise Wesley had spent his life opposing.

When Allen was dragged from his knees at St. George's Methodist Church in 1787 he did not reject Methodism. He rejected the corruption of Methodism.

What he built — the African Methodist Episcopal Church — was in many ways closer to Wesley's original vision than the white Methodist church that had ejected him. The AME kept:

- Wesley's theology of universal grace
- Wesley's insistence that faith must produce social action
- Wesley's opposition to the oppression of the poor
- The Methodist structure of worship and church governance

What it added was something Wesley could not have given it — complete Black ownership and self-determination. No white oversight. No compromise with slaveholders. No accommodation of the system Wesley had called "the sum of all villainies."

Allen finished what Wesley started.

---

## The Irony

John Wesley — a white English minister born in 1703 — built a theology that was more compatible with Black liberation than almost any other Christian tradition of his era.

His American followers compromised it within decades of his death.

A formerly enslaved Black man in Philadelphia took that same theology, stripped out the compromises, and built an institution that has survived for over 200 years and still exists today.

Wesley's original vision survived because Allen rescued it.
