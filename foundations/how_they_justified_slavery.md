# How They Justified Slavery — The Ideology of Oppression

Understanding how ordinary people convinced themselves that monstrous things were acceptable is more important than just knowing the dates. The same mechanisms operate in every system of oppression.

---

## 1441 — The First Capture

In 1441 a Portuguese explorer named **Antão Gonçalves** sailed down the West African coast and captured 12 Africans near Cape Bojador in modern day Mauritania. He brought them back to Portugal as a gift to Prince Henry the Navigator.

This was not yet a trade. It was a kidnapping. But it planted the seed.

By 1444 the Portuguese were conducting organized raids on the West African coast. On **August 8, 1444** the first large public sale of enslaved Africans took place in Lagos, Portugal. Around 235 men, women, and children were auctioned in a public square.

A Portuguese chronicler named **Gomes Eanes de Zurara** witnessed it and wrote about it — one of the earliest documented accounts of the Atlantic slave trade. Even Zurara — who supported the trade — described families being separated and children torn from mothers with discomfort. He said it made his heart heavy.

But he justified it anyway.

That tension — feeling something is wrong but justifying it — is exactly what made the system so durable.

---

## The Church — Making Evil Holy

The Catholic Church was the most powerful institution in Europe in the 15th century. Papal authority was absolute. What the Pope declared was law — spiritual and political.

**1452 — Dum Diversas**

Pope Nicholas V issued a papal bull granting Portugal the authority to:

> *"invade, search out, capture, vanquish, and subdue all Saracens and pagans whatsoever, and other enemies of Christ... and to reduce their persons to perpetual slavery."*

The Pope — God's representative on earth — explicitly authorized the permanent enslavement of non-Christians. This wasn't a loophole. This was official Church doctrine.

**1455 — Romanus Pontifex**

Pope Nicholas V followed up with another papal bull extending these rights and specifically authorizing Portugal to trade in enslaved Africans.

The Church didn't just permit slavery. It blessed it. It made it a holy enterprise — spreading Christianity through conquest. If you enslaved a pagan and exposed them to Christianity, you were saving their soul. The violence was reframed as mercy.

This gave ordinary Portuguese sailors, merchants, and nobles a complete moral framework. They weren't doing evil — they were doing God's work.

---

## The Bible as a Weapon

Beyond the papal bulls slaveholders reached into the Bible for justification.

The most used passage was the **Curse of Ham** from Genesis 9. After Noah's flood, Noah curses his son Ham's descendants to be "servants of servants." European slaveholders interpreted this as God's decree that Africans — identified as Ham's descendants — were divinely ordained to be enslaved.

This interpretation had no serious theological basis. It was invented and applied specifically to justify what they were already doing. But it was enormously powerful because:

- It gave slavery a divine origin — God ordained it, not man
- It made resistance to slavery resistance to God's will
- It gave enslaved people a framework to internalize their own subjugation — if God made you a servant, accepting that was righteous

Slaveholders selectively read scripture to enslaved people — specifically choosing passages about obedience and submission. The full Bible — including Exodus, the story of Moses leading enslaved people to freedom — was deliberately withheld in many cases.

## How the Ideology Spread Across Europe

Portugal started it but every major European power that joined the slave trade adopted and adapted the same justification framework. Here is how it spread country by country.

**Spain — 1479**
Spain entered the slave trade through the **Treaty of Alcáçovas** in 1479 which gave Portugal control of the African coast in exchange for Spain receiving the Canary Islands. Spain then accessed enslaved Africans through Portuguese traders and brought them to their colonies in the Caribbean and Americas — Cuba, Puerto Rico, Hispaniola, and the mainland. Spain used the same Church justification Portugal had — the papal bulls applied to all Catholic powers. The Spanish also developed the **encomienda system** — a legal framework that gave Spanish colonizers the right to the labor of indigenous and enslaved people in exchange for supposedly Christianizing them. Evil dressed as charity.

**Britain — 1562**
Britain entered the slave trade through **John Hawkins** — an English privateer who in 1562 hijacked a Portuguese slave ship off the coast of Africa and sold the 300 enslaved Africans in Hispaniola. He made such enormous profit that Queen Elizabeth I personally invested in his next voyage. By the late 1600s Britain had established the **Royal African Company** — a state-sanctioned monopoly on the slave trade. When the monopoly ended in 1698 and the trade was opened to all British merchants the volume exploded. Britain eventually became the largest slave trading nation in history — transporting an estimated 3.1 million Africans. Britain added a specific ideological layer — the idea of **"civilizing" Africans** through enslavement and Christianity. This became the foundation of what Rudyard Kipling would later call "the White Man's Burden" — the idea that European domination of non-white people was an act of benevolence.

**France — 1600s**
France entered formally through the **Code Noir** — the Black Code — issued by King Louis XIV in 1685. The Code Noir was a set of laws governing slavery in French colonies. It defined enslaved Africans as property, outlined punishments for resistance, and simultaneously required that all enslaved people be baptized Catholic. This was the French version of the same contradiction — enslaved people were legally property but spiritually human enough to need saving. The Code Noir was presented as a humane regulation of slavery — setting limits on the worst abuses. In reality it codified and normalized the entire system under royal law.

**The Netherlands — 1600s**
The Dutch entered through the **Dutch West India Company** founded in 1621 — a state-backed trading company that operated slave forts along the West African coast. The Dutch were largely secular in their justification — less emphasis on Christianity, more on pure commerce. They were the bankers and brokers of the slave trade, financing expeditions for other nations and profiting from the financial infrastructure of the entire system. Amsterdam became the financial capital of the Atlantic slave economy.

**Denmark — 1600s**
Denmark established slave trading posts in West Africa and operated plantations in the Danish Virgin Islands — now the US Virgin Islands. Denmark was actually the **first European country to abolish the slave trade in 1792** — though it gave a 10 year grace period before enforcement, allowing traders to maximize profits before the ban took effect. Denmark's abolition was driven less by morality and more by economic calculation — their slave colonies were becoming less profitable.

---

**The Key Pattern Across All European Powers**

Every nation that entered the slave trade followed the same three-step process:

1. **Adopt the existing justification** — the Church framework Portugal had established was already in place. New entrants didn't need to create a new ideology — they inherited one that was already institutionalized.

2. **Add their own legal framework** — Britain's Royal African Company, France's Code Noir, Spain's encomienda system. Each country created laws that normalized slavery within their specific political culture.

3. **Make it economically central** — once the trade was profitable enough that national economies depended on it, the ideology became self-reinforcing. Questioning slavery meant questioning the foundation of the entire economic order. That made the justification feel necessary rather than chosen.

By the 18th century the ideology wasn't just something individuals believed — it was built into the law, the economy, the Church, and the science of every major European power simultaneously. That is what made it so difficult to dismantle and why it took centuries to end legally — and has never fully ended in terms of its consequences.

## Pseudo-Science — The Secular Justification

As the Enlightenment developed in the 17th and 18th centuries secular reasoning gained influence. Slaveholders needed a justification that didn't rely solely on religion. They invented one — **scientific racism.**

European scientists constructed an elaborate framework claiming that human races were biologically distinct and hierarchically ordered. At the top — white Europeans. At the bottom — Africans.

**Key figures:**

**Carl Linnaeus** — Swedish botanist who created modern biological classification. In 1758 he classified humans into four categories and described Africans as "lazy, crafty, slow, careless" — character judgments presented as scientific fact.

**Johann Friedrich Blumenbach** — coined the term "Caucasian" in 1795 and created a racial hierarchy placing Caucasians at the top as the most beautiful and intelligent race.

**Samuel Morton** — American physician in the 1800s who collected hundreds of human skulls and measured cranial capacity, claiming to prove white Europeans had larger brains and greater intelligence. His methodology was later proven completely fraudulent — he unconsciously manipulated measurements to confirm what he already believed.

This was not fringe thinking. These were respected scientists at prestigious institutions producing work treated as legitimate scholarship. It gave slavery an intellectual foundation backed by the authority of science rather than God.

## How Ordinary People Didn't Feel Evil

This is the most important question. How did ordinary sailors, farmers, merchants, and politicians participate in or benefit from extraordinary evil without feeling like evil people?

**Dehumanization**
If you genuinely believe the people you are enslaving are subhuman — closer to animals than people — then owning them feels no different than owning livestock. The pseudo-science and religious justification worked together to make this believable. You can't feel guilty about enslaving someone you don't recognize as fully human.

**Distance**
Most people who benefited from slavery never saw a slave ship or a plantation. They bought sugar for their tea, wore cotton clothes, drank rum — all produced by enslaved labor — without ever confronting where it came from. Evil is easiest when you don't have to look at it directly.

**Normalization**
When something is legal, economically central, socially accepted, and religiously sanctioned — it becomes normal. Questioning it requires swimming against every current of your society simultaneously. Most people don't do that. They go along. That's not unique to slaveholders — it's human nature under systems of normalized evil.

**Economic Dependency**
By the 18th century the economies of Britain, France, Portugal, Spain, and the American colonies were so deeply dependent on enslaved labor that dismantling it would have meant economic collapse. People rationalized keeping a system they may have privately doubted because the alternative was personally catastrophic.

**The Reformers Existed Too**
There were always people who called it evil. Abolitionists made moral arguments against slavery for decades before it ended. The system didn't persist because everyone believed the justifications — it persisted because those with power and economic interest in maintaining it were willing to use violence and law to keep it going regardless.

---

## Why This Matters

Malcolm X understood this completely. He said the most dangerous person in America was not the outright racist — it was the person who participated in and benefited from a system of oppression while telling themselves they were a good person.

The ideology built to justify slavery didn't die when slavery ended. It adapted. Understanding how it was constructed is the first step to recognizing how it operates today in different forms.

The mechanisms are always the same:
1. Dehumanize the target
2. Get official sanction — religious, legal, or scientific
3. Normalize it through law and economics
4. Create enough distance so participants don't have to confront what they're doing
