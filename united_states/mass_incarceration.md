# Mass Incarceration — The New Jim Crow

---

## The Numbers

The United States has **the largest prison population in the world** — in both absolute numbers and per capita rate.

- **2.3 million** people currently incarcerated (jails + prisons)
- **4.5 million** people on probation or parole — under criminal justice supervision but not behind bars
- **70 million Americans** have a criminal record of some kind — roughly 1 in 3 adults

No other country on earth comes close. The US incarcerates more people than China — a country with 4x the population and an authoritarian government.

**The racial breakdown:**
- Black Americans are **13% of the population**
- Black Americans are **38% of the prison population**
- Black men are incarcerated at **5x the rate** of white men
- 1 in 3 Black men born today will spend time in prison at some point in his life
- For white men: 1 in 17

---

## How It Was Built — The Timeline

Mass incarceration was not a sudden development. It was built deliberately over five decades through specific policy choices.

**1971** — Nixon declares War on Drugs. Federal drug enforcement expands.

**1973** — New York's **Rockefeller Drug Laws** — among the first mandatory minimum laws in the country. Set mandatory prison sentences for drug offenses regardless of circumstances. Became the model for other states.

**1980s** — Reagan escalates the War on Drugs. Crack/powder cocaine disparity enacted. Prison population begins dramatic rise.

**1994** — Clinton Crime Bill. Three strikes laws. $9.7 billion in new prison construction. Prison population accelerates.

**1996–2000** — Private prison industry expands dramatically. **Corrections Corporation of America (now CoreCivic)** and **GEO Group** become major corporations. Their business model requires high incarceration rates.

**Result:** US prison population went from **200,000 in 1971** to **2.3 million by 2008** — an **1,150% increase** in less than 40 years.

## The 13th Amendment Connection

Read the 13th Amendment again:

> *"Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, **except as a punishment for crime**... shall exist within the United States."*

Prison labor operates under this exception. Incarcerated people across the United States work for wages ranging from **$0.13 to $0.52 per hour** in many state prison systems. Some states — Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Mississippi, South Carolina, Texas — pay incarcerated workers **nothing at all** for most labor.

The work they perform:
- Manufacturing — clothing, furniture, license plates, military equipment
- Agriculture — farming, livestock
- Services — call centers, data entry, firefighting (California uses incarcerated people as firefighters at $1/hour)
- Prison maintenance
- Government services

Major corporations that have used or currently use prison labor: **Walmart, McDonald's, Sprint, Verizon, Victoria's Secret, American Airlines, Starbucks** — through prison industry programs.

The constitutional structure that enabled convict leasing in 1865 is the same structure enabling prison labor today. The legal framework has never been closed.

## The Private Prison Industry

In 1984 the first private prison in America opened in Tennessee, operated by the newly formed Corrections Corporation of America.

Private prison companies make money based on **occupancy**. Their contracts with state governments often include **occupancy guarantees** — states must keep prisons at 80–90% capacity or pay penalties. This creates a financial incentive for high incarceration rates built into government contracts.

Private prison companies lobby actively for:
- Mandatory minimum sentencing laws
- Longer sentences
- Immigration detention (a major growth area)
- Laws that increase the scope of criminal liability

**The lobbying money:**
- CCA and GEO Group have spent over **$10 million** lobbying federal and state governments in recent years
- They donate to politicians across both parties who support tough-on-crime policies

This is an industry whose profit depends on more people being incarcerated. It has financial incentives directly opposed to criminal justice reform and actively works to prevent it.

## Collateral Consequences — The Invisible Sentence

When a person is released from prison the sentence continues through a web of legal restrictions that follow them permanently.

**Voting rights:**
- 48 states restrict voting rights for people with felony convictions in some form
- 11 states permanently disenfranchise people for some felony convictions even after completion of sentence
- **5.2 million Americans** are currently disenfranchised due to felony convictions
- 1 in 13 Black Americans of voting age is disenfranchised — compared to 1 in 56 non-Black Americans

**Employment:**
- Federal law allows employers to conduct criminal background checks and deny employment based on convictions
- "Ban the Box" legislation in some jurisdictions has limited this but not eliminated it
- Many professional licenses are denied to people with criminal records — nursing, teaching, law, real estate

**Housing:**
- Public housing authorities are permitted to deny housing to people with criminal records
- HUD regulations have allowed blanket exclusion policies
- Private landlords routinely screen out applicants with records

**Education:**
- The 1998 Higher Education Act denied federal financial aid eligibility to people with drug convictions — partially reformed but not fully eliminated
- Many colleges conduct criminal background checks in admissions

**Federal benefits:**
- The 1996 welfare reform law allows states to impose lifetime bans on SNAP (food stamps) and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families for people with drug felony convictions
- 37 states have exercised this option in some form

**Family separation:**
- Incarceration separates parents from children — more than **2.7 million American children** have an incarcerated parent
- Children with incarcerated parents have significantly elevated rates of poverty, developmental problems, and eventual incarceration themselves
- This creates a multigenerational cycle

## The School to Prison Pipeline

Mass incarceration begins before prison. The pathways that funnel Black children into the criminal justice system start in school.

**Disparities in school discipline:**
- Black students are **3.8 times more likely** to receive out-of-school suspensions than white students
- Black students with disabilities are **twice as likely** to be restrained or secluded
- Black preschoolers represent **18% of preschool enrollment** but **48% of preschool suspensions**

**Zero tolerance policies:**
School disciplinary policies introduced in the 1990s created mandatory suspension and expulsion for a broad range of behaviors. Children removed from school fall behind academically, are more likely to repeat grades, and are dramatically more likely to eventually be incarcerated.

**Police in schools:**
The presence of school resource officers (police) in schools increases the likelihood that student behavior is handled through the criminal justice system rather than school discipline. A student who gets in a fight is now more likely to be arrested than given detention.

The result is a direct pipeline from school suspension → dropout → criminal record → incarceration.

## Michelle Alexander's Framework

Law professor Michelle Alexander's *The New Jim Crow* (2010) provided the most influential framework for understanding mass incarceration as a racial control system rather than simply a crime response:

Her argument:
1. The War on Drugs created a system of mass incarceration that operates as a racial caste system
2. Once released, people with felony convictions are legally discriminated against in employment, housing, voting, and benefits — creating a permanent second-class status
3. This system functions like Jim Crow — not through explicit racial language but through ostensibly race-neutral policies applied in racially disparate ways
4. The system is self-perpetuating — disenfranchisement removes political power from the communities most affected, preventing them from voting out the politicians who maintain the system

The book was controversial among some legal scholars who argued the analogy to Jim Crow was overstated. The statistical reality it documented was not disputed.
