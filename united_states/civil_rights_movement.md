# The Civil Rights Movement — 1954 to 1968

---

## What It Actually Was

The Civil Rights Movement is taught in American schools as a triumphant moral story — peaceful protesters marching, King giving speeches, laws changing, problem solved.

That is a sanitized myth. The actual movement was:
- Dangerous — activists were murdered, beaten, bombed, and imprisoned
- Contested — there was deep disagreement within Black America about strategy and goals
- Incomplete — the legal victories did not translate into economic equality
- Actively suppressed — by local governments, state police, and the FBI simultaneously

Understanding it requires seeing it as it actually was: a mass political movement that won significant legal battles against extraordinary violence and opposition, while leaving the economic foundations of racial inequality largely intact.

---

## The Legal Foundation — Brown v. Board of Education (1954)

The NAACP's legal strategy, led by **Thurgood Marshall**, had been systematically challenging segregation in courts for decades. The culmination was *Brown v. Board of Education*.

The Supreme Court ruled **unanimously** that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional — directly overturning *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) and the "separate but equal" doctrine.

Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote: *"Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."*

The Southern response was immediate and organized. Over 100 Southern congressmen signed the **Southern Manifesto** in 1956, declaring Brown unconstitutional and pledging to resist it by all legal means. State governments passed "interposition" resolutions. School districts closed entirely rather than integrate.

**The Little Rock Crisis (1957):** Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus deployed the National Guard to prevent nine Black students from entering Central High School. President Eisenhower — who personally opposed integration — was forced by the constitutional crisis to send the **101st Airborne Division** to escort the students into school.

Brown established a legal principle. Enforcing it was another matter entirely.

## Emmett Till — 1955

Before the major campaigns there was **Emmett Till**.

Till was a 14-year-old Black boy from Chicago visiting relatives in Money, Mississippi in August 1955. He was accused of whistling at a white woman — Carolyn Bryant — at a store.

Days later her husband Roy Bryant and his half-brother J.W. Milam abducted Till from his great-uncle's home in the middle of the night, beat him, gouged out one of his eyes, shot him in the head, and threw his body in the Tallahatchie River with a 75-pound cotton gin fan tied around his neck with barbed wire.

His mother **Mamie Till-Mobley** demanded an open casket funeral in Chicago so the world could see what they had done to her son. *Jet* magazine published the photographs. Hundreds of thousands of people filed past the casket.

Bryant and Milam were acquitted by an all-white jury after 67 minutes of deliberation. Protected by double jeopardy they later admitted to the murder in a paid interview with *Look* magazine.

Emmett Till's murder and his mother's decision to make it visible galvanized the movement. **Rosa Parks** said she thought of Emmett Till when she refused to give up her seat on the Montgomery bus.

## Montgomery Bus Boycott — 1955 to 1956

On December 1, 1955 **Rosa Parks** was arrested in Montgomery, Alabama for refusing to give up her seat on a city bus to a white passenger.

Parks was not simply a tired seamstress who spontaneously refused to move. She was a trained activist and NAACP secretary who had attended the Highlander Folk School — a civil rights training center. Her arrest was the planned trigger for an organized campaign.

The **Montgomery Improvement Association** was formed. A young 26-year-old Baptist minister named **Martin Luther King Jr.** was elected its president.

For **381 days** the Black community of Montgomery — roughly 40,000 people — boycotted the city bus system. Black residents walked miles to work, organized carpools, and sustained economic pressure on the city.

The financial impact on the bus company was severe. The legal challenge went to the Supreme Court. In November 1956 the Court ruled that Montgomery's bus segregation was unconstitutional.

The boycott worked. King emerged as the movement's national leader.

## The Campaigns — 1960 to 1965

**Sit-ins — 1960**
On February 1, 1960 four Black college students from North Carolina A&T sat down at the whites-only Woolworth's lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina and refused to leave when denied service. They were verbally abused and physically attacked. They returned the next day with more students.

The sit-in tactic spread to over 50 cities within weeks. The **Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC)** was founded to coordinate student activism.

**Freedom Riders — 1961**
The Supreme Court had ruled that segregation in interstate bus terminals was unconstitutional. The federal government was not enforcing it. The **Congress of Racial Equality (CORE)** organized interracial groups of activists to ride interstate buses through the South to test enforcement.

In Anniston, Alabama a bus was firebombed. In Birmingham riders were beaten by a mob while police stood by — the FBI had informed the Birmingham police commissioner, Bull Connor, of the attack in advance. In Montgomery riders were beaten again.

The Kennedy administration was forced to act — the Interstate Commerce Commission issued regulations prohibiting segregation in interstate travel facilities.

**Birmingham — 1963**
King and the SCLC chose Birmingham specifically because of Bull Connor — they knew his brutality would be documented and force a national response.

The campaign began in April 1963. Connor responded to peaceful marches with fire hoses and police dogs against protesters including children. The images went worldwide.

The Birmingham campaign forced the Kennedy administration to introduce what became the Civil Rights Act. **Medgar Evers** — NAACP field secretary in Mississippi — was assassinated in his driveway the night Kennedy announced the legislation.

**March on Washington — August 28, 1963**
250,000 people gathered at the National Mall. King delivered the "I Have a Dream" speech.

What is rarely taught: the march's original agenda, organized partly by **Bayard Rustin** and **A. Philip Randolph**, included demands for economic justice — jobs and wages — not just civil rights. Those demands were softened under pressure from the Kennedy administration before the march.

**Civil Rights Act — 1964**
Prohibited discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in employment and public accommodations. Banned unequal application of voter registration requirements.

Kennedy was assassinated in November 1963. Lyndon Johnson pushed the legislation through Congress.

**Selma and the Voting Rights Act — 1965**
Black residents of Selma, Alabama attempted to march to Montgomery to demand voting rights. On **Bloody Sunday** — March 7, 1965 — 600 marchers crossing the Edmund Pettus Bridge were met by state troopers who attacked them with clubs and tear gas on live national television.

The footage shocked the country. Johnson went before Congress and used the words of the movement: *"We shall overcome."*

The **Voting Rights Act of 1965** prohibited discriminatory voting practices and required federal oversight of elections in states with a history of discrimination. Black voter registration in the South jumped dramatically within years.

## What the Movement Did Not Fix

The Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act were genuine victories. They dismantled the legal apparatus of Jim Crow.

They did not touch:
- The **wealth gap** — built over centuries and entrenched through redlining and GI Bill exclusion
- **Residential segregation** — the Fair Housing Act of 1968 prohibited discrimination but did not undo decades of redlining
- **School funding inequality** — schools funded by local property taxes meant Black neighborhoods with lower property values had worse schools
- **Economic inequality** — desegregating lunch counters did not change who owned the businesses

King understood this. By 1966–1968 he was explicitly talking about economic justice — not just legal equality. He opposed the Vietnam War, costing him the support of the Johnson administration. He was organizing the **Poor People's Campaign** — a multiracial economic justice movement demanding federal jobs programs and guaranteed income. He was planning a massive occupation of Washington DC when he was killed.

The sanitized version of King — the "I Have a Dream" King — was frozen at 1963. The King who was evolving toward a structural economic critique of American capitalism is largely erased from the mainstream narrative.

## The Internal Debate — King vs. Malcolm X

The civil rights movement was not monolithic. The central tension was between King's integrationist, nonviolent approach and Malcolm X's black nationalist, self-defense oriented approach.

**King argued:**
- Nonviolence was morally correct and strategically effective — it exposed the violence of the system
- Black Americans should demand full inclusion in American democracy
- Coalition with white allies was necessary and possible
- The goal was integration and equal citizenship

**Malcolm argued:**
- Nonviolence in the face of violence was suicidal
- Integration into a corrupt system was not liberation
- Black people needed self-determination, not white approval
- The goal was Black control of Black communities

These were not simply tactical disagreements. They were fundamentally different analyses of what Black liberation required.

Both men were evolving at the time of their deaths. Malcolm toward a broader human rights framework. King toward a structural economic critique. We do not know where either would have arrived.
