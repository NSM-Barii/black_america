# The War on Drugs — Racial Policy Dressed as Public Health

---

## The Admission

In 1994 John Ehrlichman — Nixon's domestic policy chief and a Watergate felon — gave an interview to journalist Dan Baum. The interview was not published until 2016, after Ehrlichman's death. In it he said:

> *"The Nixon campaign in 1968, and the Nixon White House after that, had two enemies: the antiwar left and Black people. You understand what I'm saying? We knew we couldn't make it illegal to be either against the war or Black, but by getting the public to associate the hippies with marijuana and Blacks with heroin, and then criminalizing both heavily, we could disrupt those communities. We could arrest their leaders, raid their homes, break up their meetings, and vilify them night after night on the evening news. Did we know we were lying about the drugs? Of course we did."*

This is not a theory or an allegation. This is a confession from the man who designed the program, in his own words, explaining the explicit racial and political intent behind the War on Drugs.

Nixon declared the War on Drugs in **1971**.

---

## How It Was Built

**Nixon (1971–1974)**
- Declared drug abuse "public enemy number one"
- Dramatically expanded federal drug enforcement agencies
- Created the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) in 1973
- Scheduled marijuana as a Schedule I drug — the most restrictive category — despite his own commission recommending decriminalization

**Reagan (1981–1989)**
- Dramatically escalated the War on Drugs
- Mandatory minimum sentencing — fixed prison sentences for drug offenses regardless of circumstances
- Zero tolerance policies
- The **Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986** — the legislation that created the crack/powder cocaine disparity

**The Crack vs. Powder Disparity — The Smoking Gun**

The 1986 law mandated:
- **5 grams of crack cocaine** triggered a 5-year mandatory minimum sentence
- **500 grams of powder cocaine** triggered the same 5-year sentence

A **100:1 ratio** in sentencing for the same drug in two different forms.

The two forms of cocaine are pharmacologically nearly identical. The difference:
- **Crack** was cheap — $5 to $20 per rock. Predominantly used in poor urban Black communities
- **Powder** was expensive. Predominantly used by white and wealthy users

The law was not based on any pharmacological or public health evidence showing crack was 100 times more dangerous than powder. It was based on racially coded media coverage of the "crack epidemic" that portrayed it as a uniquely Black problem requiring an extreme criminal response.

The result: **Black people received dramatically longer sentences than white people for equivalent drug use.**

The disparity was reduced to **18:1 by the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010** — still not equal, and not retroactive for people already serving sentences under the 100:1 ratio.

**Clinton and the 1994 Crime Bill**
The Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994 — the largest crime bill in American history — included:
- The "three strikes" provision — mandatory life sentence for a third felony conviction
- $9.7 billion in new prison funding — incentivizing mass incarceration
- Elimination of Pell Grants for incarcerated people — cutting off education as a rehabilitation path
- Expansion of the federal death penalty

Joe Biden was the Senate sponsor. Bill Clinton signed it. Both have since expressed regret about its effects.

---

## Who Was Targeted

The War on Drugs was applied in racially disparate ways from the beginning.

**The data:**
- Black and white Americans use illegal drugs at **roughly equal rates**
- Black Americans are **3.73 times more likely** to be arrested for marijuana possession than white Americans despite similar usage rates
- Black Americans represent **29% of drug arrests** but **13% of the population** and roughly 13% of drug users
- Black defendants in federal drug cases receive sentences on average **19% longer** than white defendants convicted of the same offenses

This is not explained by drug use patterns. It is explained by where police deploy, who they stop, who they prosecute, and how they sentence.

---

## The Prison Pipeline

The War on Drugs was the primary engine of **mass incarceration** in America.

In 1971 — when Nixon declared the War on Drugs — the US prison population was approximately **200,000**.

By 2008 — after four decades of escalating drug policy — it was **2.3 million**. The largest prison population in the world, in both absolute numbers and per capita.

The increase was driven primarily by drug offenses — particularly nonviolent drug possession. The communities that bore the weight of this were Black and Latino communities in urban areas.

A Black child born in the 1990s had a **1 in 3 chance** of spending time in prison at some point in his life. For white children it was 1 in 17.

---

## The Collateral Consequences

A drug conviction — even for a nonviolent offense, even after sentence is served — carries lifetime consequences:

- **Disenfranchisement** — in many states a felony conviction means permanent loss of voting rights
- **Employment** — "have you ever been convicted of a felony?" on nearly every job application
- **Housing** — public housing can be denied to people with drug convictions; private landlords routinely screen out applicants with records
- **Education** — federal student loan eligibility affected by drug convictions
- **Public benefits** — federal law allows states to ban people with drug felonies from receiving food stamps and welfare
- **Child custody** — a conviction can be used against a parent in custody proceedings

A single nonviolent drug conviction can follow a person — and their family — for the rest of their life. This is by design.

---

## Why It Has Not Been Dismantled

The War on Drugs has been acknowledged as a failure by public health experts, criminologists, economists, and increasingly by politicians across the political spectrum.

It has not been dismantled because:

1. **It is profitable** — private prison companies, bail bond companies, drug testing companies, and law enforcement agencies all have financial interests in maintaining high incarceration rates
2. **It is political** — "tough on crime" rhetoric has been politically effective for decades; being seen as "soft on crime" is perceived as electoral poison
3. **It disenfranchises its targets** — people with felony convictions often cannot vote, reducing the political power of the communities most harmed by the policy
4. **The 13th Amendment exception still exists** — prison labor is constitutionally legal; an incarcerated population is an exploitable labor force
