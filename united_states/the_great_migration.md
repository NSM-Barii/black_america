# The Great Migration — 1910 to 1970

---

## What It Was

Between 1910 and 1970 approximately **6 million Black Americans** left the South and relocated to cities in the North, Midwest, and West.

This was the largest internal migration in American history. It transformed the demographic, cultural, and political map of the United States permanently.

It happened in two waves:

- **First Wave: 1910–1940** — approximately 1.6 million people, driven by World War I labor demand and the intensification of Jim Crow terror
- **Second Wave: 1940–1970** — approximately 5 million people, driven by World War II labor demand and continued racial violence

---

## Why They Left

Three forces combined to push Black Southerners north:

**1. Terror**

Jim Crow was not just inconvenient — it was lethal. Lynching, convict leasing, debt peonage, and the constant threat of violence for any perceived violation of racial social codes made the South genuinely dangerous to live in.

The **Red Summer of 1919** — a wave of white supremacist riots and massacres across the country including Chicago, Tulsa (1921), and dozens of other cities — demonstrated that the North was not safe either. But it was less systematically lethal.

**2. Economic Exploitation**

Debt peonage and sharecropping kept Black families locked in poverty regardless of how hard they worked. The economic system was designed to prevent Black wealth accumulation. The North offered industrial wages — actual cash wages — that sharecropping never would.

**3. Labor Demand**

World War I and World War II created enormous demand for industrial labor in Northern cities. Steel mills, meatpacking plants, auto plants, munitions factories — all needed workers. Immigration from Europe had been curtailed. Northern industrial employers began actively recruiting Black Southern workers.

The **Chicago Defender** — a Black newspaper with national distribution — published stories about Northern opportunity and actively encouraged migration. It was smuggled into the South where its distribution was sometimes suppressed by white authorities.

---

## Where They Went

The migration was not random. It followed specific routes and concentrated in specific cities:

- **Chicago** — the primary destination from Mississippi and the Deep South; the Black population of Chicago grew from 44,000 in 1910 to over 800,000 by 1970
- **Detroit** — auto industry; Black population grew from 6,000 in 1910 to 660,000 by 1970
- **New York (Harlem)** — drew migrants from the Southeast and the Caribbean
- **Philadelphia** — second major East Coast destination
- **Los Angeles** — West Coast destination, grew dramatically in the second wave
- **Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Milwaukee** — industrial cities across the Midwest

---

## What They Found

The North was not the promised land. It was different — but it had constructed its own system of racial exclusion.

**Housing:**
Redlining and racial housing covenants confined Black migrants to specific overcrowded urban neighborhoods. In Chicago, the area available to Black residents was called the **Black Belt** — a narrow strip of the South Side where the entire Black population was compressed regardless of income.

Landlords charged premium rents for deteriorating housing because Black renters had no alternatives. Overcrowding was extreme. When Black families attempted to buy homes outside designated Black areas they faced violence — homes were bombed, families were threatened, mobs attacked. This happened in Chicago, Detroit, and other northern cities routinely.

**Labor:**
Industrial jobs paid real wages — a genuine improvement over sharecropping. But:
- Unions frequently excluded Black workers from skilled trades
- Black workers were confined to the dirtiest and most dangerous jobs
- They were last hired and first fired
- During the Depression Black workers lost jobs at dramatically higher rates than white workers

**Violence:**
The North had no formal lynching culture but had extensive police brutality and race riots initiated by white residents defending neighborhood boundaries.

The **Chicago Race Riot of 1919** — triggered when a Black teenager swimming in Lake Michigan drifted into an informally "white" section of the beach and was stoned and drowned — lasted 13 days and killed 38 people (23 Black, 15 white), injured 537, and left 1,000 Black families homeless.

**De facto segregation:**
Northern segregation was enforced through custom, economic practice, and violence rather than law. The result was the same — Black communities concentrated in specific neighborhoods, attending underfunded schools, excluded from economic opportunity.

---

## What the Migration Built — The Cultural Impact

Despite the conditions Black migrants encountered, they built something transformative.

**The Harlem Renaissance (1920s–1930s)**
Harlem became the cultural capital of Black America. Writers, artists, musicians, intellectuals, and activists concentrated in one neighborhood and produced an outpouring of Black art, literature, and thought:
- Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Claude McKay — literature
- Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong — music
- Aaron Douglas — visual art
- The intellectual ferment that produced the Black nationalism debates between Garvey and Du Bois

**Chicago Blues**
Mississippi Delta blues musicians migrated to Chicago and electrified their sound — Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, Little Walter. Chicago blues became the foundation of rock and roll.

**Detroit Motown**
Berry Gordy founded Motown Records in Detroit in 1959 — a Black owned record label that produced Marvin Gaye, Stevie Wonder, Diana Ross, the Temptations, the Four Tops. Motown crossed over into white audiences and changed American popular music permanently.

**Political Power**
Concentrated in Northern cities, Black Americans became a significant political voting bloc for the first time. This political power — particularly in swing states like Illinois, Michigan, and New York — gave Black voters leverage they had never had in the South and contributed to the political pressure that eventually produced civil rights legislation.

---

## The Reverse Migration

Since the 1970s there has been a **reverse migration** — Black Americans moving back to the South, particularly to Atlanta, Charlotte, Houston, and other New South cities. The South's economy transformed, Jim Crow was legally dismantled, and Southern cities offered more affordable housing and stronger Black cultural institutions than Northern cities experiencing deindustrialization.

As of recent census data more Black Americans live in the South than at any point since the Great Migration began.
