# Jim Crow — Legal Apartheid in America

---

## What Jim Crow Was

Jim Crow was not one law. It was a **system** — a comprehensive legal and social architecture of racial segregation enforced by state and local governments across the South and in varying degrees across the entire country.

It lasted from the end of Reconstruction (1877) until the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Nearly **90 years** of legally enforced racial hierarchy.

The name came from a minstrel show character — a caricature of Black people performed by white actors in blackface. The name itself was a statement of contempt.

---

## Plessy v. Ferguson — 1896

The legal foundation of Jim Crow was established by the US Supreme Court in **Plessy v. Ferguson** (1896).

Homer Plessy — a man who was 1/8 Black — deliberately sat in a whites-only railroad car in Louisiana to test the state's Separate Car Act. He was arrested. The case went to the Supreme Court.

The Court ruled **7-1** that racial segregation was constitutional as long as the separate facilities were "equal." This became the **"separate but equal"** doctrine.

It was a lie from the moment it was written. The facilities were never equal. The Court knew they wouldn't be. "Separate but equal" was legal cover for a system of deliberate racial subordination.

Justice John Marshall Harlan wrote the lone dissent:

> *"Our Constitution is color-blind and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens."*

He was ignored for 58 years until Brown v. Board of Education overturned Plessy in 1954.

---

## What Segregation Actually Looked Like

Jim Crow laws mandated separation in virtually every area of public life:

- Schools
- Hospitals — Black patients turned away from white hospitals, died in underfunded Black hospitals
- Restaurants
- Hotels
- Drinking fountains
- Bathrooms
- Swimming pools — many cities closed public pools entirely rather than integrate them
- Libraries
- Parks
- Cemeteries — you could not be buried next to white people
- Courtrooms — Black witnesses swore on a separate Bible
- Prisons — segregated
- Mental institutions — segregated
- Telephone booths in some jurisdictions

This was not informal social custom. These were **laws** with criminal penalties for violation. A Black person sitting at the wrong lunch counter could be arrested.

## Disenfranchisement — Removing the Vote

After the 15th Amendment guaranteed Black men the right to vote, Southern states developed an arsenal of methods to eliminate it in practice while technically not violating the amendment:

**Poll taxes** — a fee required to vote. Designed to price out poor Black voters.

**Literacy tests** — administered selectively and subjectively. A Black man with a college degree could be failed. An illiterate white man could pass. Registrars had complete discretion.

**Grandfather clauses** — allowed men to vote without meeting other requirements if their grandfather had voted. Since formerly enslaved people's grandfathers had been enslaved and unable to vote, this excluded virtually all Black men.

**White primaries** — the Democratic Party in the South declared itself a private organization and held whites-only primary elections. Since the South was a one-party region, winning the Democratic primary was equivalent to winning the election. Black voters were locked out of the only election that mattered.

**Voter intimidation** — showing up to register could mean losing your job, your home, or your life.

The result: **Black voter registration in Mississippi dropped from 67% in 1867 to less than 6% by 1900.**

## Lynching — Terror as Policy

Lynching was not random mob violence. It was a systematic tool of racial terror used to enforce the social order of white supremacy.

**The numbers:**
- Over **4,000 documented lynchings** of Black people in the South between 1877 and 1950
- This is the documented minimum — the actual number was higher
- Mississippi, Georgia, and Texas had the highest numbers

**What lynching was used to enforce:**
- Any challenge to white authority — economic, political, or social
- Accusations of crime — most often unsubstantiated
- "Violations" of racial social codes — looking at a white woman, speaking assertively to a white person, owning land that white people wanted
- Voting or attempting to vote
- Business success that made white competitors uncomfortable

Lynchings were frequently public spectacles. Crowds gathered. Photographs were taken and sold as postcards. In some cases lynchings were advertised in local newspapers in advance.

This was not the behavior of isolated extremists operating against social norms. This was the social norm, endorsed by local governments and law enforcement that consistently refused to prosecute.

**Ida B. Wells** — a Black journalist — documented lynching systematically in the 1890s and published the data at enormous personal risk. She was run out of Memphis after her newspaper office was destroyed. She continued her work from the North. Her investigative journalism was the first serious effort to document and publicize the scale of lynching in America.

**The federal anti-lynching bill** was introduced in Congress repeatedly from 1900 onward. It was blocked by Southern senators for **decades**. The US did not pass a federal anti-lynching law until **2022** — the Emmett Till Antilynching Act.

## Sundown Towns

Across the North, Midwest, and West — not just the South — thousands of towns operated as **sundown towns**: communities where Black people were not permitted to be present after dark.

Signs at city limits read: *"N*****, don't let the sun set on you here."*

These were enforced by police and by violence. Black travelers, workers, and families had to navigate a country where hundreds of towns were effectively closed to them.

Historian James Loewen documented over **10,000 sundown towns** across America in his research. This was not a Southern phenomenon. Illinois, Ohio, Oregon, California — all had sundown towns.

## Economic Exclusion — Closing the Wealth Gap Permanently

Jim Crow didn't just restrict Black people socially. It systematically excluded them from every major wealth-building opportunity of the 20th century.

**The GI Bill (1944)**
The Servicemen's Readjustment Act provided returning World War II veterans with college tuition, low-interest home loans, and business loans. It created the American middle class.

Black veterans were technically eligible. In practice:
- Southern universities refused Black applicants
- Banks refused to process home loans for Black veterans in white neighborhoods
- The VA administered benefits through local offices controlled by white Southern officials who denied or delayed Black veterans' claims
- Of the first 67,000 mortgages insured by the GI Bill in New York and New Jersey, **fewer than 100** went to non-white veterans

The GI Bill built generational white wealth. Black veterans who fought in the same war were systematically excluded from it.

**Redlining**
The Federal Housing Administration — created in 1934 — developed maps of American cities color-coded by perceived mortgage risk. Black neighborhoods were colored red — "hazardous." Banks were instructed not to provide mortgages in these areas.

Redlining made it impossible for Black families to buy homes in most of the country and impossible for those who did own homes in Black neighborhoods to access equity or refinancing. It locked Black families out of the primary wealth-building mechanism of the 20th century — home ownership.

The maps were used until the **Fair Housing Act of 1968**. The neighborhoods they destroyed have not recovered.

---

## The North Was Not Free

This requires emphasis: **Jim Crow was a national system, not just a Southern one.**

The North had:
- Sundown towns
- Redlining
- Discriminatory union practices that excluded Black workers from skilled trades
- School segregation through residential segregation created by redlining
- Police violence against Black communities
- Housing covenants — contractual agreements attached to property deeds that prohibited sale to Black buyers

The difference was that Northern segregation operated largely without explicit legal mandate — through custom, contract, and economic practice rather than state law. The effect was the same.

When the civil rights movement won legal victories against Southern Jim Crow and turned north, it encountered this reality. Martin Luther King Jr. led marches in **Chicago in 1966** and was met with some of the most violent white resistance he had encountered anywhere in the country. He said the hatred in Chicago was worse than anything he had seen in Alabama.
